    Designing machine learning systems

    Quote

    Machine learning engineering is 10% machine learning and 90% engineering.

    Chip Huyen, author of Designing Machine Learning Systems

    Building machine learning systems is not just about training models and deploying them. The success of an ML system depends heavily on its design process — how well you understand the problem, define the objectives and plan the system architecture before writing a single line of code.

    As ML engineers and practitioners, it is tempting to dive straight into the data and algorithms. However, failing to think through the design can lead to systems that are brittle, inefficient or simply incapable of meeting real-world requirements. Said in another way, we want to minimize the amount of rework that we have to do and the overall technical debt that we accumulate.

    In addition, you are often not the only stakeholder in an ML project. Data engineers, data scientists, product managers and business stakeholders all need a shared picture of what is being built. A clear design that can be communicated to both technical and non-technical people is therefore crucial.

    If you want to go deeper on this topic after the module, we highly recommend Chip Huyen's book Designing Machine Learning Systems: An Iterative Process for Production-Ready Applications, which gives a fantastic overview of the processes that go into designing modern machine learning systems.

    Product design vs systems design

    Before we start developing any machine learning models, we need to motivate and design the application. A useful way to think about this (taken from the excellent Made With ML course) is to split design into two phases:

    Product design (What & Why)  →  Systems design (How)
    • Product design asks what we are building and why. Who are the users? What pain are we removing? What does success look like in measurable terms? Is machine learning even the right approach, or would a heuristic do?
    • Systems design asks how we engineer that product end-to-end: data, metrics, modeling principles, inference, feedback and the surrounding project constraints. Made With ML covers this in their systems design lesson.

    This course spends most of its time on the operations side of MLOps — containers, CI/CD, cloud, deployment, monitoring and so on. Those tools only pay off if you know what system you are trying to build. This module is about getting that picture in place before you pick tools.

    Thinking in systems

    A useful mental model for production ML is a closed loop:

    data → model → inference → decision → the real world → new data / feedback

    If you only optimize the middle (the model), you ignore the parts that usually decide whether the project survives: who acts on the prediction, how long labels take to arrive, what happens when the world drifts, and who is hurt when the model is wrong.

    A few ideas that show up again and again in well-designed ML systems:

    • Tradeoffs are inevitable. Latency vs accuracy, batch vs online inference, interpretability vs performance, freshness vs cost. Write them down instead of discovering them in production.
    • Delays matter. Labels may arrive weeks after a prediction. Retraining takes time. Drift detection always lags reality. Your design should say how the system behaves while you wait.
    • Feedback can be dangerous. Predictions change user behavior, which changes the data you train on next. This is one of the classic sources of hidden technical debt in ML systems.
    • Local optima are easy. A model with great offline accuracy and no plan for serving, monitoring or decision rights is still a bad production system.

    You do not need to become a systems-theory expert. You do need the habit of asking what sits around the model.

    The MLOps canvas

    To make the design process concrete, we use an MLOps canvas developed for this course. It is inspired by the Business Model Canvas, the Machine Learning Canvas by Louis Dorard, and the Made With ML canvas, but tilted towards the questions that show up when you actually try to operate ML in production.

    Download / open these two PDFs:

    • One-page fillable canvas — what you fill out
    • Full guide with questions and references — prompts for every box

    The canvas has six columns that you should fill left to right:

    Column Boxes What you are forcing yourself to answer
    Problem Background, Value Proposition, Objectives Why does this exist, and how do we know we succeeded?
    Data Data Collection, Data Verification and Governance Where does data come from, and can we trust it?
    Model Modelling, Metrics and Evaluation, Model Governance How do we build, judge and version the model?
    Operations Inference, Decision How are predictions produced and acted on?
    Monitoring Feedback, Lifetime How do we learn after launch, and when do we retrain or retire?
    Risk Fairness, Explainability, Security What can go wrong ethically, socially or adversarially?

    The header fields (Product name, Designed by, Date, Iteration) are not decoration. Treat the canvas as a living document: bump the iteration number every time your understanding of the system changes in a meaningful way.

    How to fill the canvas without getting stuck

    • Prefer short, concrete sentences over slogans. "Reduce 30-day readmissions by 20%" beats "improve patient care".
    • On the first pass, finish the Problem and Data columns properly and only stub the later boxes.
    • Do not write tool names on the first pass (Docker, MLflow, FastAPI, ...). Describe what needs to happen; you will map tools onto the boxes later in the course.
    • Leaving Monitoring or Risk blank is itself a design smell. An open question is fine; silence is not.

    Worked example: inventory demand forecasting

    Below is a compressed example of how a filled canvas might look for a retail inventory problem. Your own answers will be shorter or longer depending on the project — the point is the kind of questions each box answers.

    Retail company — inventory management

    Problem

    Background
    XYZ Retail overstocks some products and stock-outs others, which wastes warehouse space and loses sales. Store managers currently order from gut feeling plus a crude moving average.
    Value proposition
    Better demand forecasts should reduce holding costs while keeping popular products available. Managers still make the final order decision; the model recommends quantities.
    Objectives
    Primary — reduce inventory holding cost by 15% while keeping product availability at 98%. Secondary — improve forecast accuracy by 20% vs the current baseline on a held-out period.

    Data

    Data collection
    Daily sales, inventory levels and deliveries from the ERP; promotion calendars; store metadata. Labels for "true demand" are tricky during stock-outs (you only observe sales). Need a policy for censored demand.
    Data verification and governance
    Schema checks on daily extracts; access limited to the inventory analytics team; no customer-level PII in the training tables. Document known biases (new stores, COVID years, one-off viral products).

    Model

    Modelling
    Start with a strong baseline (seasonal naive / gradient-boosted trees on lag features) before any deep sequence model. Features include lags, prices, promotions, holidays and store type.
    Metrics and evaluation
    Offline: MAE / RMSE and bias per store-category. Online / business: stock-out rate, overstock rate, holding cost. Slice metrics by store size and product velocity so a good average does not hide bad segments.
    Model governance
    Models versioned; staging vs production; weekly retrain candidate compared against the champion on a replay window before promotion.

    Operations

    Inference
    Nightly batch forecasts for the next 14 days, written back into the ordering system before the morning planning meeting. Latency is not critical; freshness and reliability are.
    Decision
    Recommendations appear as suggested order quantities. Managers can override; overrides are logged. Fully automatic ordering is out of scope for v1.

    Monitoring

    Feedback
    Compare forecast vs realized sales; track override rate (high override often means the model is not trusted or not useful). Use a small holdout of stores/products that do not influence training immediately, to detect harmful feedback loops.
    Lifetime
    Expect seasonal retrain; trigger review if MAE worsens by X% for two consecutive weeks or if a category's stock-out rate spikes. Plan for retiring models that no longer beat the baseline.

    Risk

    Fairness
    Avoid systematically under-serving smaller or rural stores if training data is dominated by flagship locations.
    Explainability
    Managers need simple reasons for a recommendation (promotion next week, last year spike, ...), not only a number.
    Security
    Protect commercial sales data; control who can push a model to production; watch for poisoned or manually edited training extracts.

    Connecting the canvas to the rest of the course

    One reason for introducing the canvas early is that the rest of the course fills it with concrete tools and practice. As you go through the sessions, you should be able to point at a canvas box and say which module made that box real:

    Canvas focus Where you will meet it in the course
    Data collection, versioning, reproducibility Organisation, data version control, experiment tracking
    Modelling, metrics, debugging Config/code structure, debugging, testing
    Model governance, automation CI/CD, cloud workflows
    Inference and decisions APIs, deployment, frontends
    Feedback, lifetime, drift Monitoring and data drifting
    Fairness, explainability, security Threaded through design, data governance and deployment choices

    You do not need to fill every box perfectly on day one. You do want a first draft that you can revise when you know more.

    ❔ Exercises

    Do these in a group of 3–4 if you can. Design work is easier (and more realistic) when someone can disagree with you.

    1. Skim the Made With ML product design lesson (and optionally the systems design lesson). In your own words, what is the difference between product design and systems design? Why does Made With ML put product design before model development?

      Solution

      Product design covers what to build and why (users, value, objectives, feasibility). Systems design covers how to engineer it (data, metrics, modeling principles, inference, feedback). Doing product design first reduces the risk of building a technically impressive model that nobody needed, or optimizing the wrong metric.

    2. Open the one-page canvas and the question guide. Pick one of the scenarios below (or use your course project if you already have one). Fill:

      • All three Problem boxes
      • Both Data boxes
      • At least one box from each of Model, Operations, Monitoring and Risk

      Time-box yourselves: roughly 5 minutes per box on this first pass. No tool names.

      Scenario A — hospital readmissions

      A hospital wants to predict which patients are at high risk of being readmitted within 30 days of discharge, so care teams can schedule follow-ups. Clinicians must stay in the loop for care decisions. Patient data is sensitive (think GDPR / health regulation). Labels arrive when readmission does or does not happen within 30 days.

      Scenario B — EV charging network

      An energy company wants to decide where to place new charging stations and how to staff maintenance. They have usage logs from existing stations, traffic estimates and demographic data. City permits and grid capacity constrain what is possible. Wrong placement wastes capital for years.

      Scenario C — content moderation assist

      A community platform wants a model that flags potentially rule-breaking posts for human moderators. False positives annoy users; false negatives cause harm. Attackers may try to evade the model. Moderators disagree with each other, so labels are noisy.

    3. On a piece of paper or a whiteboard, draw the closed loop for your scenario:

      data → model → inference → decision → world → feedback

      Mark one delay (for example label latency) and one feedback risk (how acting on predictions could change future data).

    4. Swap canvases with another group. Spend 10 minutes on a red-team pass. Your job is not to be nice:

      • Cross out any Objective that is not measurable
      • Add a sticky note where Decision rights are unclear (who can override the model?)
      • Name one way the system can fail quietly for weeks without anyone noticing
      • Check that Risk is not empty

    5. Write down three tradeoffs for your scenario (for example batch vs online inference, precision vs recall, automation vs human approval). For each tradeoff, note which canvas box captures it.

    6. Without looking at the course schedule, guess which later modules will help you make the Operations and Monitoring columns more concrete. Then check your guess against the table in the previous section.

    7. (Optional, recommended for project groups) Put the canvas (or a photo/export of it) into your project repository next to the project description. Set Iteration to 1. Plan a revisit after you have worked on deployment and monitoring: update the boxes that were hand-wavy and bump the iteration number.
